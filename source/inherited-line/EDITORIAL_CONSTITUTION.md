# Editorial Constitution

## Arabic Through Stories — The Permanent Editorial Law of the Reader Series

*Companion to PROJECT_CHARTER.md. The Charter states what we build and why; this Constitution states how every reader must be designed, written, edited, and evaluated.*

---

## Standing Provisions

**SP-1 — Scope.** This Constitution governs every reader produced by the program, at every level, indefinitely. It governs the series, not any single book. Level specifications, production workflows, and individual reader plans are subordinate documents: they may refine these rules for their scope, but may not contradict them without a recorded, approved exception.

**SP-2 — The governing standard.** Stories must be stories, never target structures disguised as stories. A reading that satisfies every language rule in this Constitution and fails this standard is rejected. No reading is approved merely because it contains its targets.

**SP-3 — The dual-justification rule.** Every sentence in every reader must be justified twice: it must do work for the story and work for the language. A sentence that exists only to exhibit a structure is a defect. A sentence that cannot be understood at level is a defect. Neither justification excuses the absence of the other.

**SP-4 — The five protected principles.** All editorial work protects, in order of application: meaning first; high repetition; strong contextual and visual support; story context; delayed output. No editorial decision may sacrifice one of these to convenience.

**SP-5 — Terminology discipline.** The terms defined in this Constitution — *core target*, *recycled target*, *support vocabulary*, *recognition-only*, *controlled dialogue chunk*, *binding*, *approved*, *licensed* — are used precisely and only as defined. "Approved" describes only what a recorded decision has approved, within the scope that decision states. Status labels are never carried forward on assumption; they are confirmed against the current record.

**SP-6 — Honesty of record.** A required fact that is missing is marked as a gap and surfaced, never invented. Two active sources that contradict on a point affecting production are flagged as a source conflict, and work on the conflicted point halts until a human decision resolves it and the resolution is recorded. No proposed rule is silently treated as approved; no gap is silently treated as filled.

# Part I — Source Authority

## 1.1 Primary Curricular Authorities

Two external resources are the program's permanent curricular authorities. Neither may be overridden by preference, convenience, or story ambition.

### 1.1.1 The Frequency-Based Dictionary

The adopted frequency dictionary (currently Buckwalter & Parkinson, *A Frequency Dictionary of Arabic*, Routledge) **governs vocabulary**. Specifically, it governs:

- **Selection priority.** Vocabulary enters the series in broad alignment with documented frequency. High-frequency items are presumptively eligible; low-frequency items are presumptively ineligible and must earn admission under §3.3.
- **Evidence.** Every frequency claim made anywhere in the program must cite the dictionary at lemma level: rank, location, and match type. No rank may be asserted that has not been verified against the source. Fabricated or remembered ranks are prohibited.
- **Lemma discipline.** Evidence attaches to the dictionary's own lemmatization. A related noun is never cited as evidence for a verb; homographs are separated explicitly; an inflected teaching form is recorded as an inflected form of its ranked lemma, not as an independently ranked item.
- **The chunk rule.** A multiword teaching frame is never itself called a ranked corpus item. Frequency evidence belongs to the frame's lexical head or heads, and the record must say so.
- **Register screening.** Dictionary entries are screened for register. Dialect equivalents and sub-standard spellings are recorded as separate lemmas and excluded from MSA evidence.

The dictionary **proposes; it does not dispose**. Frequency is one criterion alongside communicative usefulness, comprehensibility at level, story utility, visualizability, load discipline, recycling value, and naturalness. No item is admitted merely because its rank is high, and no numerical frequency cutoff exists unless one is explicitly approved.

### 1.1.2 The Grammar Guide

The adopted learner grammar (currently Alhawary, *Modern Standard Arabic Grammar: A Learner's Guide*) **governs grammatical progression**. Specifically, it governs:

- **Sequence.** Grammatical structures enter the series in an order consistent with the guide's account of learner-appropriate progression. No structure may be introduced substantially ahead of that progression without a recorded, approved exception.
- **Complexity.** The guide's treatment of a structure's difficulty informs when and how the structure may appear, and what may be expected of learners once it has.
- **Cumulative development.** The progression is cumulative: each reader assumes the grammatical ground of the readers before it, and no more.
- **Accuracy.** The guide is the reference of first resort for questions of correct MSA form.

The Grammar Guide governs *what may appear and when*. It does not govern *how grammar is experienced*: that is fixed by Part IV of this Constitution, and no reading of the guide may convert a reader into a grammar lesson.

### 1.1.3 Neither authority invents the series

The two authorities constrain and evidence the progression; they do not design it. The program's own recorded curricular decisions allocate specific items to specific readers. Where the program's allocation and an authority appear to conflict, the conflict is surfaced under SP-6, not resolved by improvisation.

## 1.2 Editorial Authorities

### 1.2.1 Hierarchy

Internal documents bind in the following order, highest first:

1. **The Project Charter** — the constitutional vision. Nothing may contradict it.
2. **This Editorial Constitution** — the permanent editorial law.
3. **Binding governance documents** — recorded standards ratified by the program, in their own declared precedence order where one exists.
4. **Recorded editorial decisions and rulings** — gate decisions, approved exceptions, allocation rulings. Only recorded decisions change a status, a target set, or an open question.
5. **Level specifications and approved planning baselines** — bind planning within their scope; they do not by themselves authorize production.
6. **Reader-specific plans, briefs, and QC records** — bind the reader they govern.

Lower documents may refine higher ones; they may not contradict them. An exception to a higher rule is valid only when explicitly documented and approved at the level the rule requires.

### 1.2.2 Roles

- **Governance documents** state binding standards. A governance document that is still in draft may not be cited as binding by anyone for any purpose.
- **Editorial decisions** are the sole mechanism by which statuses change. A decision exists when it is recorded — with its scope, its rationale where required, and its decider. Verbal, relayed, or remembered approvals are not recorded decisions. Every approval statement must state what it authorizes *and what it does not authorize*; approval scope is never extended by implication.
- **Quality-control standards** define the checks every reader must survive (Part X). QC documents record findings and approvals; they do not create new rules. A QC record that identifies a rule gap surfaces it for decision.
- **Reader samples** — published readers of this series and reference stories — are *evidence of practice*, not authority. A sample demonstrates how a rule has been satisfied well; it cannot amend a rule, and a defect in a published reader is never precedent for repeating the defect. Samples predating a rule change are not models for new work.
- **External references** (CI/TPRS literature, other publishers' series, research) are advisory only. They inform judgment; they are never citable as project authority, and craft advice drawn from them must be labeled as external.

### 1.2.3 Conflict resolution

When two active sources contradict on a point that affects production:

1. Name both sources and the exact point of conflict.
2. Halt work that depends on the conflicted point.
3. Apply the hierarchy of §1.2.1 as the interim reading where work must be triaged, but treat the conflict as open until resolved.
4. Escalate to the responsible human decision-maker; the resolution is recorded, and the losing document is amended or annotated so the conflict cannot recur silently.

Discovering a conflict is a contribution, not a nuisance. Suppressing one is an editorial offense.

# Part II — Reader Architecture

## 2.1 What constitutes a reader

A **reader** is a complete, self-sufficient book: a titled work of connected Arabic prose (and controlled dialogue), written within a defined language envelope, that a learner at the intended level can read from cover to cover with comprehension and pleasure, without a teacher, a dictionary, or an external curriculum. A reader always contains at least one full narrative arc and closes with either an earned resolution or a deliberate, safe irresolution.

A reader is *not*: a bank of disconnected passages; a workbook with stories attached; a graded anthology of exercises; or a serialized grammar syllabus. If the organizing logic of the book is a language inventory rather than a reading experience, it is not a reader under this Constitution.

## 2.2 What constitutes a chapter

A **chapter** (or unit-level reading) is a titled, learner-facing passage of connected prose meant to be read as a text, carrying its own narrative movement: something is wanted, attempted, discovered, or changed within it. Every chapter both stands as a satisfying reading in itself and advances the book's larger arc or reading sequence.

Not chapters, and never counted as readings: isolated example sentences; captions; dialogue fragments used only for activities; vocabulary boxes; word-family or root-recognition lists; activity instructions; teacher-facing text.

## 2.3 Readers are not textbooks

The structural consequences of the Charter's reader/textbook distinction are binding:

- The table of contents of a reader lists *story*, not *language points*.
- No chapter exists because "the syllabus has an item." Chapters exist because the story has a next movement.
- Explicit language support (glossing, noticing features, activities) is physically and functionally subordinate to the reading text: it follows the reading, never precedes or interrupts it, and never becomes the reason a page exists.
- Nothing in a reader presupposes classroom delivery. Teacher materials, where produced, are separate products and may not colonize the reader's pages.

## 2.4 Allowable reader structures

No single format is mandatory. A reader may be, among other defensible shapes:

- a single continuous story told across chapters;
- an episodic book — one cast, self-contained episodes;
- a linked collection — distinct stories sharing a world, theme, or language envelope;
- a core-story architecture — a central narrative supported by parallel and contrasting readings that recycle its language in genuinely different stories;
- at higher levels, mixed forms including nonfiction narrative and informational text, where the level specification admits them.

Whatever the structure, four architectural principles bind:

1. **Every reading earns its slot.** Each reading has a distinct narrative and acquisitional function. Readings are never added to raise word count, showcase language, or fill a template — and a reading count ceiling set by a level specification may not be evaded by relabeling readings as "texts," "reviews," "boxes," or "extensions."
2. **Order serves acquisition.** Within a reader, sequence moves from supported encounter toward independent reading and, only then, guided response: support → input → variation → retelling → response.
3. **Repetition and novelty are distributed, not crowded.** The architecture assigns repetition (familiar language, familiar story) and novelty (new engines, new contexts) to different readings so that no single reading must deliver both at once.
4. **The book must feel like a book.** Front matter, design, and apparatus follow trade publishing conventions for the intended readership, not workbook conventions.

## 2.5 Allowable chapter structures

Chapters may vary in length, dialogue density, and internal shape within the level's planning bands. Binding principles:

- Every chapter changes something: a chapter after which the story's state is unchanged is a filler chapter and is rejected.
- Word counts are planning bands, never quotas. A shorter, complete, causal chapter always beats a longer padded one; if a chapter needs filler to reach its band, the band is wrong, not the chapter.
- A chapter's language load (new vocabulary, new structure, new characters, new setting) is planned before drafting and audited after (Parts III, IV, VII).
- Cliff-ends and open questions between chapters are legitimate and encouraged; incoherence between chapters is not.

# Part III — Vocabulary System

## 3.1 Vocabulary categories

Every Arabic item in a reader carries **exactly one** status, assigned in the reader's language inventory before drafting. Statuses do not drift: no item's status changes except by a recorded inventory decision.

| Category | Definition | Budget effect | Permitted role |
|---|---|---|---|
| **New core target** | An item this reader newly teaches; an acquisition objective | Consumes the new-target budget | Full plot-bearing use; incurs recycling obligations (§3.2.5) |
| **Recycled target** | A core target introduced in an earlier reader | Free | Full use; counts as established language |
| **Support vocabulary** | Established or transparent language that scaffolds meaning without being an objective | Free | Props, settings, scaffolding; **may not carry the causal chain of the plot** |
| **Recognition-only vocabulary** | Ambient language the learner meets but is never held to | Free | Ambient presence; never assessed, never required for comprehension of plot, never silently promoted |
| **Controlled dialogue chunk** | A fixed phrase admitted so dialogue can sound natural despite containing forms outside the productive set | Free | Fixed, listed use only; never analyzed morphologically; never productive unless separately approved |
| **Function vocabulary** | Connectors, particles, prepositions, pronouns, negators | Enters as a core target or as a listed chunk — never unclassified | Because function words restructure everything around them, they are introduced deliberately, one at a time, and never ride in unnoticed |
| **Proper nouns** | Names of characters and places | Excluded from all counts and percentages | Chosen for decoding ease and visual distinctness; spelled and vocalized consistently; never used to smuggle vocabulary |
| **Low-frequency narrative vocabulary** | A rare item a specific story wants | Admissible only as support or recognition-only, with recorded justification | Never a core target absent an approved exception; **never admitted to add excitement the story should generate itself** |

Two anti-inflation rules protect the system:

- **Appearance does not confer status.** A support word used forty times is still support. Nothing becomes a target by being present.
- **Promotion is a decision.** Elevating any support, recognition, or chunk item to core status is a recorded curricular decision that triggers a full recycling path from the promotion point onward.

## 3.2 Vocabulary introduction

**3.2.1 Selection.** Vocabulary is selected by weighing, together: documented frequency (§3.3); communicative usefulness; semantic transparency; learner readiness; recycling opportunity; and the needs of approved stories. Selection is recorded in the reader's inventory with its justification. If a story idea requires unapproved vocabulary, **the story changes, not the inventory** — inventory amendments happen at planning level, never as drafting expedients.

**3.2.2 Quantity.** Each reader carries a small, planned budget of new core targets, set by its level specification within the program's normal range (for early levels, on the order of a handful per reader-unit — the range is a planning norm, not a floor). A below-range budget is permitted with recorded rationale; an above-range budget is not permitted. New vocabulary never arrives in clusters: no passage may front-load unfamiliar items faster than context can support them.

**3.2.3 Contextual introduction.** A new item's first appearance is inside meaningful story context — an event where its meaning is recoverable from what surrounds it (narrative situation, established language, illustration). First encounters never occur in lists, glosses, activities, headings, or ambient text. Support precedes input: the reader meets the situation before the word must carry it alone.

**3.2.4 Repetition.** Every new core target recurs within its own reader — in the core narrative and across other readings, in **genuinely distinct narrative functions** where the item allows. Recurrence counts only when it does work: the test for a real occurrence is that *removing the sentence would change the story's meaning, a character's action, or the reader's understanding.* Listed, glossed, verbatim-copied, or activity-only appearances do not count. Repetition must remain natural prose; written text that imitates oral drilling is rejected.

**3.2.5 Recycling.** Recycling is an obligation assumed at introduction, on three horizons:

- **Immediate** — within the introducing reader, per §3.2.4.
- **Short-delay** — meaningful reappearance in the next reader or two where story logic permits. "Story logic permits" is a documented judgment: a skip is recorded with a reason and a scheduled make-up; repeated skips escalate to review of the item's viability.
- **Spaced** — periodic reappearance across the series thereafter, verified at planned sweep points. An item that cannot find a natural spaced slot repeatedly becomes a **retirement candidate** — it is never forced into an unnatural sentence to satisfy the ledger.

Retirement of an item from active duty occurs only by explicit recorded decision, never by silence.

**3.2.6 The established-language share.** Every reading is written mostly in language the learner already owns. Each level specification fixes a measured band for the recycled-or-transparent share of every reading (the program's founding band is 70–85%); the band's floor protects comprehensibility and its **ceiling is equally binding** — a reading above it is a restatement of owned language, not a bonus. The share is a *measured Stage-QC result computed on final prose by the approved method, never a planning claim*. Out-of-band readings return to revision or carry a recorded, approved exception. The sanctioned repair for an over-ceiling reading is adding genuine new content, not deleting recycled content.

**3.2.7 Cumulative growth.** The series behaves as one growing lexicon. Every reader's available language is the union of all prior introductions (as tracked in the ledger), plus its own budget. No reader may assume vocabulary the ledger does not establish; no reader may ignore the vocabulary the ledger does.

## 3.3 Frequency policy

**3.3.1 Priority.** The default source of new core vocabulary is the high-frequency core of the language as documented in the Frequency-Based Dictionary. Rank order is not a queue — the series does not introduce words 1 through N mechanically — but sustained divergence from the frequency core requires sustained justification.

**3.3.2 Evidence and documentation.** Every core target carries a frequency record: the dictionary lemma, rank, location, match type (lexical match, component match, within-entry documentation, related form), and any qualification (homograph separation, register note, inflected-form note). Chunks record the evidence of their lexical heads and are never themselves called ranked items. Items admitted without frequency evidence (permissible only by recorded decision) are recorded as exactly that — the gap is documented, not backfilled.

**3.3.3 Exceptions.** Two exception classes exist, both recorded:

- **Utility admissions** — an item of modest rank admitted primarily on story or communicative utility, labeled as such with the rank recorded honestly.
- **Affordance admissions** — an item admitted because the narrative system requires it (e.g., story-mechanical verbs), with the basis recorded and no frequency claim invented for it.

**3.3.4 Lexical vs. narrative usefulness.** Both are required over the span of a reader, and neither substitutes for the other. A high-rank item with no story use in the planning horizon waits. A story-useful item with poor frequency standing enters, if at all, under §3.3.3 or as support/recognition. The vocabulary that defines the series is the intersection: frequent items that stories keep needing.

**3.3.5 No arbitrary selection.** Every Arabic item in every learner-facing text traces to an authorization: the reader's approved inventory, established prior-series language per the ledger, or a listed chunk. An item with no traceable authorization is a defect wherever it appears, and "it is common," "it is useful," or "the story wanted it" is not an authorization.

## 3.4 The Vocabulary Ledger

The program maintains one **Vocabulary Ledger** for the entire series — the single continuous record of what learners own.

- **One record per item**, keyed to the dictionary lemma where one exists, holding: current status; introducing reader; status history with the recorded decision behind every change (introduction, promotion, retirement); frequency evidence per §3.3.2; and licensed forms per §4.2.
- **Appearance tracking.** The record logs the item's meaningful appearances by reader, sufficient to verify the three recycling horizons of §3.2.5.
- **Update discipline.** The ledger is updated as part of each reader's quality control, from final approved prose — never from plans. A reader is not approved while its ledger updates are outstanding.
- **Authority.** For the question "what language may this reader assume?", the ledger is the operative record. Planning from memory, from an old reader's text, or from an unapproved draft is prohibited.
- **Honesty.** The ledger records what is, including skips, gaps, and retirement candidacies. A ledger curated to look healthy is worse than no ledger.

# Part IV — Grammar System

## 4.1 The Grammar Guide's governance

Grammatical progression across the series is planned against the Grammar Guide:

- **Introduction.** A structure becomes a candidate for introduction when the guide's progression and the learners' accumulated ground make it appropriate — and it is actually introduced only when an approved story naturally requires it. The story requirement is real: a structure whose turn has come waits if no planned story needs it.
- **Sequencing.** Structures enter in learner-appropriate order per the guide. No structure appears substantially ahead of sequence without a recorded exception; nothing in any reader depends on a structure the sequence has not yet delivered.
- **Progression and cumulative development.** Each reader's grammatical envelope is the cumulative set of introduced structures plus its own small planned additions. Complexity rises by deliberate, recorded steps — a new clause type, a new connector, a new frame is an *event* in the series, planned and traceable, never a drift.
- **Recycling.** Introduced structures remain in active service in subsequent readers, on the same three horizons as vocabulary (§3.2.5), tracked in the Grammar Ledger.

## 4.2 Grammar categories

| Category | Definition | Discipline |
|---|---|---|
| **New grammar target** | A structure this reader introduces as an acquisition objective | Counts toward the reader's planned structural load; incurs recycling obligations |
| **Recycled grammar** | Structures introduced earlier, now established | Free use within their licensed forms |
| **Support grammar** | Grammar present inside fixed chunks (greetings, dialogue formulas, set frames) that the learner meets whole | Used only as the fixed chunk; never analyzed, never extended, never assessed as grammar |
| **Receptive grammar** | Structures admitted input-first: learners meet and understand them, but no production is expected | Explicitly marked input-first in the inventory; conversion to productive expectation is a separate recorded decision |

**Form-level licensing.** A licensed form licenses only itself. Authorization of one person, tense, number, polarity, or pronoun form of an item does not extend to any other form; each form is separately licensed or deferred, and no authorization is ever inferred from the separate presence of individual tokens elsewhere.

**Phrase-level integrity.** Where a target is a phrase or frame, the phrase is the unit of authorization: it appears whole, with its components never detached, isolated, or recombined outside the licensed shape.

**Exclusion discipline.** Each level specification lists structure classes excluded by design at that level. Excluded structures require an approved amendment to enter — they do not enter by being useful, natural, or tempting.

## 4.3 Grammar exposure

How grammar is experienced is fixed, at every level of the series:

- **Repeated exposure.** A new structure recurs many times in its introducing reader and thereafter — always inside sentences doing narrative work. The recurrence-counting rule of §3.2.4 applies to structures identically.
- **Natural exposure.** Structures appear where a fluent writer would use them. Grammar never forces the text: no scene, sentence, or line of dialogue exists to exhibit a structure, and no text is contorted to avoid a structure the story genuinely needs (the sanctioned instrument for that situation is the controlled chunk, §3.1).
- **Contextual exposure.** Structures are met inside meaning already established — the learner always knows *what* is happening when meeting a new way of saying it.
- **Cumulative exposure.** Old structures do not vanish when new ones arrive; every reader exercises the accumulated grammar of the series to date.

**Prohibited modes of exposure**, at all levels, in all learner-facing products: conjugation tables and paradigms; grammatical terminology; parsing and case-ending instruction; translation drills; rules detached from story examples; any explicit feature that precedes the structure's first meaningful appearances in reading. Where a level admits light noticing support, it comes after input, carries one focus at a time, uses story language for its examples, and returns the learner immediately to meaning.

## 4.4 The Grammar Ledger

The program maintains one **Grammar Ledger** for the series, parallel in discipline to the Vocabulary Ledger:

- one record per structure: its Grammar Guide reference; introducing reader; the exact forms licensed (per §4.2), each with its own status; input-first markings and their conversion decisions; exclusion or deferral rulings;
- appearance tracking sufficient to verify recycling across the three horizons;
- updates only at reader QC, from final prose; approvals blocked while updates are outstanding;
- the same authority and honesty rules as §3.4: the Grammar Ledger is the operative answer to "what structure may this reader use, in which forms?", and it records reality, not aspiration.

# Part V — Language Standards

## 5.1 The variety

The language of the series is **Modern Standard Arabic**: accurate, natural, contemporary MSA appropriate to written narrative.

- No dialect forms, dialect lexemes, or dialect spellings appear unless a recorded decision explicitly governs a specific, labeled use. Accidental register mixing is a defect.
- Sub-standard spellings are defects everywhere, including activities and apparatus.
- A dialect or colloquial cognate is never evidence for, or a substitute form of, an MSA item.

## 5.2 Acceptable simplification

Simplification is achieved by **narrowing choices, not by breaking the language**:

- Permitted: shorter sentences; controlled lexicon; restricted structure per the grammatical envelope; higher-than-literary redundancy of the structural kind (event shown in text and image; language re-met across readings); simple clause architecture; generous vocalization.
- Not permitted: ungrammatical Arabic; unidiomatic Arabic; omission of obligatory agreement or marking; "easified" word order no fluent writer would produce; telegraphic prose.

The test: a fluent, educated reader of Arabic opening any page should find the prose *simple*, never *wrong* — recognizably real Arabic that happens to be easy.

## 5.3 Morphology and agreement

- Every form is morphologically accurate and individually licensed (§4.2). Person, number, gender, tense, and polarity are never extended by analogy from a licensed neighbor.
- Agreement — gender, number, definiteness, verb–subject, noun–adjective — is correct everywhere, including in chunks, captions, activities, and titles.
- Both masculine and feminine licensed forms occur in genuine narrative contexts across a reader, not as token concessions.

## 5.4 Orthography and vocalization

- Vocalization follows the level specification. Where full vocalization is required, it is **accurate and deliberate**: correct short vowels, shadda, sukun, and tanwin where relevant — never decorative, never partial by accident.
- Vocalization is never removed to make text "look authentic." Reduction of vocalization across the series is a planned, level-governed progression in which unvocalized encounters are recognition extensions of already-familiar, supported, unambiguous items — never a learner's first encounter with a word, and never applied where ambiguity results.
- Names are spelled and vocalized consistently across a reader and across the series.
- Approved Arabic text is **frozen at approval**: every downstream use reproduces it byte-exactly from the approved source. Retyping approved Arabic is prohibited (it silently corrupts diacritic sequencing), and all electronic search against vocalized text uses normalization-aware, diacritic-insensitive matching.

## 5.5 Punctuation and layout

- Arabic punctuation conventions, including Arabic quotation marks for dialogue, applied consistently series-wide.
- Right-to-left layout preserves word boundaries, punctuation clarity, and clean line-breaking. No layout choice may make a sentence harder to parse.

## 5.6 Idiomatic language

Idiomatic where appropriate, transparent where necessary: set phrases, formulas, and natural collocations are welcome — entering as targets or as listed chunks like all language — and are preferred over stilted literal assemblies. An idiom whose meaning cannot be made recoverable at level is deferred, not glossed into place.

## 5.7 Readability and complexity

- **Sentence complexity** is graded: early-series prose defaults to short, single-clause, single-proposition sentences; multi-clause architecture arrives as a planned grammatical event and expands gradually. At every level, the sentence is only as complex as its reader's envelope.
- **Discourse complexity** is graded with the same deliberateness: connectors are introduced one at a time and connector chains, extended reference, and paragraph-level cohesion devices arrive as planned steps. Referential distance stays short enough that pronouns and ellipsis never create puzzles.
- Information pacing: one new thing at a time. A sentence introduces a new item *or* a new twist, not both; a page's new-information load respects the reader's planning caps.

## 5.8 Dialogue

- Dialogue is natural, limited, and functional (Part VI). Its Arabic obeys every rule above.
- Direct address is handled honestly: where natural dialogue needs forms outside the productive envelope, the controlled-chunk mechanism (§3.1) is used and recorded. Contorting a scene into unnatural third-person narration to dodge direct address is a defect; so is inventing unlicensed address forms.
- Quoted speech uses authorized language only. Each distinct addressee form is a listed chunk.
- A question inside character dialogue is a character's question, never a comprehension drill in costume.

## 5.9 Unacceptable Arabic

The following are defects wherever they occur, at any level:

translationese and structural calques from English or any other language; dialect leakage absent explicit governance; sub-standard spellings; morphologically inaccurate or unlicensed forms; agreement errors; decorative, partial, or inaccurate vocalization; ambiguity created by removed vocalization; unnatural word order adopted for pedagogical convenience; drill-like repetition in prose; rare or literary vocabulary deployed for excitement; register incoherence within a text; inconsistent name orthography; and any Arabic, anywhere in any product, that cannot be traced to an authorization.

# Part VI — Story Standards

## 6.1 Story logic

Every story in the series exhibits, at the scale its level permits:

- **A clear goal.** An identifiable protagonist wants, needs, fears, or lacks something concrete, and the reader knows it early.
- **A real obstacle.** Something specific stands between the protagonist and the goal. An obstacle that costs nothing is decoration.
- **A causal chain.** Every event issues from a prior event, a decision, or an established trait. The required spine at full scale: desire → obstacle → attempt → complication or failure → decision or discovery → earned outcome. Shorter forms may compress the spine; nothing may break it.
- **Believable motivation.** Characters act for reasons on the page. No action occurs because the plot needs it.
- **Meaningful consequences.** Attempts change the situation — success and failure both leave the world different. A story whose middle could be deleted without changing its end has no middle.
- **Logical progression.** No random rescues; no unmotivated transfers of objects or affections; no forced bargains or hidden transactions; no coincidences doing load-bearing work; no filler scenes; no repetitive plot loops.

Interest is built from cause and effect, emotion, choice, consequence, and payoff — never from rare vocabulary, and never from spectacle the language envelope cannot actually narrate.

## 6.2 Character standards

- **Consistency.** A character's traits, wants, and manner persist unless an on-page event changes them — and such changes are the story's business, shown, not asserted.
- **Knowledge.** Characters know only what they have seen, heard, or been told within the text. No character acts on information the reader cannot trace to its source.
- **Motivation.** Motive is established in text (or unambiguous supported context) before or as it drives action. Writers may not *infer* motive into a story — an interior state not evidenced on the page does not exist.
- **Emotional logic.** Emotion is a readout of plot state, and it is earned: distress appears only after its cause is textually established; relief and joy appear only after the specific problem has an observable, textual resolution; no character is assigned an emotion without a resolved (or unresolved) personal stake of their own. Emotion never substitutes for causation, and stories do not close on an unearned mood.
- **Possession.** Who owns, holds, and carries what is explicit and continuously trackable — in text and in the visual plan. Objects do not teleport; transfers are motivated events.
- **Relationships.** Relationships are named simply, kept few, and kept stable within a story. Character economy is a virtue: early-level stories carry one or two named characters, and no character exists whose removal would cost the story nothing.
- **Casts rotate.** Continuity across the series lives in the *language*, not in recurring casts: stories must never depend on the learner's memory of a previous book's characters. Recurring characters, where a level permits them, are a bonus for those who remember, never a prerequisite for those who don't.
- **Respect.** All portrayals — of people, families, communities, and cultures — are respectful, age-appropriate, and non-stereotyped. Nonhuman and fantastical characters earn their place by the same story logic as everyone else.

## 6.3 Narrative progression

- **Tension** is built from information asymmetry deliberately managed: the writer decides who knows what, and when the reader finds out. Withholding is a designed act — and it binds the illustrations too, which may never reveal a development before the text does.
- **Curiosity** is a deliverable. Every reading is planned with an explicit answer to "why does the reader continue?" — an open question, a pending attempt, an unresolved want. If the answer is "they are studious," the reading fails.
- **Discovery** — information that changes the next action — is the engine of the middle of a story. Each significant dialogue turn or event either advances, blocks, redirects, or reprices the goal.
- **Reversal** is welcome and level-appropriate: failed plans, surprising answers, reframed events. A reversal must be caused, not conjured.
- **Development** across a story is real: attempts differ *because of what earlier attempts revealed*. A second attempt that is the first attempt with a new noun is the noun-swap failure inside one story.
- **Resolution** is earned or deliberately withheld. The permitted ending palette is wide — surprise, relief, reversal, realization, disappointment redeemed, new manageable problem, moral consequence, safe open question — and endings vary across any reader and across the series. Uniform happy endings are prohibited as a default; an unresolved ending is legitimate when chosen and safe at level.

## 6.4 Compelling input

Compellingness and comprehensibility are one design problem, not two:

- **The story pays for the repetition.** Acquisition requires many encounters with the same language; only narrative variety makes that repetition invisible. Structure and vocabulary are re-met because the *story* needs them again — in new events, new engines, new stakes.
- **Comprehension is the price of tension.** A confused reader feels no suspense. Causal clarity, character economy, and information pacing are what make emotional engagement possible at all — the plot itself is comprehension support.
- **Engines change; nouns don't merely swap.** Across any reader's readings and across the series, stories vary in narrative engine — the shape of desire, obstacle, and payoff — not merely in surface content. *New name + new object + same plot* is a rejected story wherever it appears.
- **The removal test.** A story retold in the learner's own language, with no reference to its language goals, must still be worth retelling. If the story only works as language practice, it does not work.

# Part VII — Reader Planning

## 7.1 The Reader Design Brief

No reader enters drafting without an approved **Reader Design Brief**. The brief is the reader's founding record, and drafting against an unapproved brief is prohibited. Required contents:

1. **Metadata** — working title; level; position in the series; intended reader profile; planned extent.
2. **Language inventory** — every Arabic item the reader will use, each with exactly one status (§3.1), and the authorization each traces to.
3. **Vocabulary targets** — the new core items, with frequency records (§3.3.2), selection rationale, and planned first-encounter contexts.
4. **Grammar targets** — the new structures, with Grammar Guide references, exact licensed forms, and input-first markings where applicable.
5. **Recycled language** — the prior-series vocabulary and grammar this reader will actively service, drawn from the ledgers, including any due recycling obligations it discharges.
6. **Language budget** — the new-target counts against level norms (with recorded rationale for any lower load); planned word-count bands; the established-share band in force; per-reader caps (new settings, new named characters, new connectors, and similar) as the level specification defines.
7. **Story concept** — protagonist, goal, obstacle, stakes, engine, and payoff type for each story; the explicit reason learners will want to continue; confirmation that the inventory can naturally express the plot's pivotal actions (if it cannot, the inventory or the concept is revised at brief stage — never patched during drafting).
8. **Chapter outline** — the reading-by-reading arc, each reading's narrative function and its acquisitional function.
9. **Activity philosophy** — what the reader's apparatus will and will not do, within Part VIII.
10. **Linguistic risks** — known hazards recorded honestly: forms that normalize identically to banned forms; visual ambiguities; chunk dependencies; anticipated pressure points on the envelope; any source gaps, labeled as such.

The brief is reviewed for concreteness. The gate questions are answered in specifics — *what does the protagonist want; what exactly prevents it; what do they do; what information changes the next action; what visibly changes by the end* — and a vague answer returns the brief, because vagueness at brief stage becomes flatness at prose stage. Self-assessments in the brief are honest; an inflated brief is a governance failure, not optimism.

## 7.2 Chapter planning

Every chapter is drafted against its own approved **chapter plan**. Required contents:

1. **Narrative objective** — what this chapter changes in the story's state, and the open question it hands to the next chapter.
2. **New vocabulary** — which core targets first appear here, and the context of each first encounter.
3. **Recycled vocabulary** — which established items this chapter services, and in what narrative function.
4. **Support vocabulary** — the support and recognition items in play, confirmed against the inventory.
5. **Target grammar** — which new structures appear, in which licensed forms, in which planned frames.
6. **Recycled grammar** — the established structures this chapter exercises.
7. **Activity objectives** — what the chapter's activities (if any) will reinforce, within Part VIII.

No chapter is drafted without an approved chapter plan; no chapter plan is approved that cannot state its narrative objective in one concrete sentence. Discovered mid-draft necessities are handled by returning to the plan, never by silent addition.

# Part VIII — Activities

## 8.1 Standing principles

1. **Input precedes production, always.** No activity asks the learner to produce, personalize, or create with language they have not first met repeatedly in meaningful reading.
2. **Activities reinforce; they never introduce.** Activities practice language the reader has already encountered in this reader or owns from prior readers. Introducing substantial new language in an activity is a defect — including in instructions, examples, and distractors.
3. **Activities serve the reading.** Every activity links to a story, an image, a chunk, or a reading. A defensible reader with no activities is preferable to a reader whose apparatus outweighs its stories.
4. **Both dimensions are practiced.** Across a reader's apparatus, activities collectively service both its target vocabulary and its target grammar — through meaning, not through analysis.
5. **The output staircase governs sequence.** Learner response ascends: recognize → choose → match → sequence → complete → retell with support → create with constraints. No activity sits above the staircase's current step for that point in the reader, and unsupported free production is never the first ask.

## 8.2 Activity domains

- **Vocabulary practice** — retrieval of meaning in context: matching to images or situations, choosing within story frames, completion from established language. Never translation lists, never isolated-word tests.
- **Grammar practice** — structures exercised inside meaning: sequencing events, completing story sentences, choosing what a character would say. Never paradigm work, never terminology, never parsing.
- **Comprehension** — questions about goals, causes, feelings, and outcomes; ordering; identifying who did what and why. Comprehension work rewards understanding the story, not scanning for tokens; it never quizzes trivia.
- **Noticing** — where the level admits it: brief, meaning-anchored attention to a pattern the learner has already met in reading; one focus at a time; story examples only; immediate return to meaning.
- **Retrieval** — spaced re-encounter of earlier language in new contexts: review readings, recombination, comparison across stories. Retrieval uses fresh contexts, never verbatim copies of earlier items.
- **Controlled production** — completion within frames, guided retelling with visual or sentence support, choice among authorized formulations.
- **Guided production** — at the top of the staircase and only after its lower steps: constrained creation (a parallel story from a scaffold, a retell from images) using established language, with support proportional to level.

Activity difficulty, load, and count respect the level specification's caps. Activities are assessed by what they cause the learner to *re-meet and mean*, not by their variety or cleverness.

# Part IX — Reader Progression

Progression across the series is governed by five permanent standards:

**9.1 Cumulative vocabulary.** Each reader's lexical ground is the ledger-verified union of all prior introductions. Vocabulary compounds: nothing taught is abandoned (absent recorded retirement), and nothing untaught is assumed. The learner mid-series always reads mostly owned language stretched by a planned margin of new.

**9.2 Cumulative grammar.** Identically for structure: each reader exercises the accumulated grammar of the series and adds its own small planned increment, in Grammar Guide order. No reader skips ground; no reader re-teaches ground as if new.

**9.3 Increasing linguistic complexity — gradually, on every dimension at once.** Sentence architecture, discourse cohesion, morphological range, dialogue sophistication, and lexical texture all rise across the series in small coordinated steps. **No dimension may spike.** In particular, no reader becomes harder by dumping unfamiliar vocabulary; difficulty increments are planned, distributed, and small enough that no learner moving from reader N to reader N+1 meets a wall.

**9.4 Increasing narrative sophistication.** Story architecture matures in step with language: from single-strand concrete plots toward multi-attempt structures, subplots, perspective, inference demands, and eventually stylistic variety and nonfiction forms — each an intentional, level-governed step. Narrative sophistication never outruns the language envelope's ability to narrate it clearly.

**9.5 Increasing learner independence.** Support fades only as strength arrives, on the level-governed schedule: vocalization reduces (§5.4), visual support thins, repetition density relaxes, predictability loosens, apparatus lightens — each fade earned by demonstrated prior acquisition, never imposed for the appearance of authenticity. The end state is a reader who needs no graded support at all; every fade is a step toward that reader, taken exactly when it can be afforded.

Progression is verified, not presumed: each reader's QC includes a progression audit against its predecessors (Part X), and any discontinuity found is a defect of the new reader, not of the series.

# Part X — Quality Control

## 10.1 Standing QC provisions

- **Every reader undergoes formal editorial review before approval.** No exceptions, including for experienced authors, revised editions, and late-series readers.
- **Measured, not estimated.** Every quantitative claim in QC — shares, counts, coverage — is computed on final prose by the approved method and reported with its inputs. Planning estimates satisfy nothing.
- **Assert effects, not artifacts.** A check verifies the property itself, never a proxy that can drift (a banner, a label, a filename). A gate that can be satisfied by editing the gate is not a gate. Automated checks are themselves tested — including positive controls proving they pass what they must pass.
- **Machines supplement; humans decide.** Automated gates handle what is mechanically checkable and are documented as to what they *cannot* check; every limitation of a machine gate is an explicit human-QC obligation. Final approval is a recorded human decision.
- **Severity is graded**: *blocker* (cannot proceed), *major* (must revise before approval), *minor* (revise before publication where feasible), *note* (suggestion). A reader is never approved with an open blocker, and every approval records what remains open at lower severities.
- **Findings return work to the earliest stage that can fix it** — a prose defect rooted in a brief defect returns to the brief; later-stage assets never repair earlier-stage decisions.
- **The remaining-risks register.** Every approval lists its unresolved concerns explicitly, each with an owner or a required decision. An approval with an empty risks register and known risks is a false record.

## 10.2 Vocabulary audit

- **Authorization** — every Arabic item in the final text traces to the inventory, the ledgers, or a listed chunk; any untraceable item is a blocker.
- **Status integrity** — every item carries its assigned status; no drift, no silent promotion; support does not carry plot; recognition items are not required for comprehension.
- **Frequency alignment** — every frequency record checks out against the dictionary at lemma level: rank, location, match type, homograph and register qualifications; no frame cited as a ranked item; recorded evidence gaps intact, not backfilled.
- **Recycling** — the established-language share measured per reading against the band, with numerator, denominator, and method reported; new-target recurrence verified across readings and narrative functions under the counting rule; due recycling obligations from prior readers verified discharged or formally skipped.
- **Lexical consistency** — items used in their established senses; names consistent; caps (settings, connectors, and similar) respected.

## 10.3 Grammar audit

- **Guide alignment** — every structure in the text is within the reader's envelope; new structures match the brief; nothing appears ahead of sequence without its recorded exception.
- **Licensing** — every form individually licensed; no inferred person, tense, number, or polarity extensions; phrase targets whole; excluded-by-design structures absent; homograph hazards (licensed and banned forms that look identical) checked by a human.
- **Recycling** — recycled structures present and functional per the plan; ledger updates prepared.
- **Morphology and agreement** — full §5.3 compliance across text, dialogue, chunks, and apparatus.

## 10.4 Arabic audit

- **Naturalness** — reads as real MSA; no calques; no drill cadence; register coherent; dialogue speakable.
- **Syntax** — word order natural; complexity within level; no pedagogical contortions.
- **Readability** — sentence and information pacing per §5.7; vocalization accurate and complete per level; layout and punctuation per §5.5; no ambiguity introduced by orthographic choices.
- **Idiomaticity** — set phrases natural and authorized; nothing stilted where an authorized natural formulation exists.
- **Text integrity** — final Arabic frozen; downstream reproductions byte-exact; all searches used normalization-aware matching.

## 10.5 Story audit

- **Rejection sweep first.** One finding among the following stops the reading regardless of other merits: target sentences disguised as story; dialogue that does not change the next action; random rescue; unmotivated transfer; forced bargain or hidden transaction; generic everyone-is-happy ending; padding; plot movement carried only by images.
- **Causal logic** — the spine present and unbroken; motivations on the page; consequences real; the independence test passed where the reading must stand alone (goal, obstacle, attempt, change, and outcome all identifiable from the text itself).
- **Compellingness** — the continuation reason identifiable in the text; information asymmetry managed; payoff earned and varied against the reader's other endings; scored against the program's narrative quality gate with thresholds as the level specification sets them, honestly.
- **Character consistency** — knowledge, motive, possession, and trait continuity verified scene by scene.
- **Emotional logic** — every emotion earned per §6.2; no unearned closing mood.
- **Chapter progression** — every chapter changes something; the arc coheres; no filler, no loops; parallel readings genuinely distinct in engine.

## 10.6 Activities audit

- **Vocabulary coverage** — target vocabulary serviced across the apparatus, in meaning-bearing tasks.
- **Grammar coverage** — target structures serviced without analysis, terminology, or paradigms.
- **Progression** — the output staircase respected; no production ahead of input; support proportional to position in the reader.
- **Level appropriateness** — no substantial new language anywhere in the apparatus, instructions included; load within caps; nothing tests trivia, speed, or spelling perfection; nothing rewards prior literacy privilege.

## 10.7 Progression audit (series-level)

Each reader is audited against its predecessors: assumed language ledger-verified; difficulty increments within tolerance on every dimension of §9.3; support fades matched to the schedule; recycling obligations across the series boundary discharged. The ledgers are updated and the reader's records archived as part of approval.

# Part XI — Editorial Principles

The following principles are permanent editorial law. They resolve the everyday cases the detailed rules cannot enumerate.

1. **Story before everything.** Story before exercises, story before grammar, story before coverage, story before cleverness. When any interest competes with the story working as a story, the story wins — and if the story cannot win within the rules, the story is redesigned, not the rules.
2. **Every sentence earns its place twice** (SP-3). The unit of editorial judgment is the sentence, and the question is always double: what is this doing for the story, and what is it doing for the language?
3. **Grammar supports communication; it never summons it.** Structures appear because meaning needs them. The moment a scene exists for its grammar, the scene is cut.
4. **If the story needs unapproved language, change the story.** The inventory is amended at planning level by decision — never at the keyboard by need.
5. **Appearing is not teaching, and appearing is not targeting.** Presence in text confers no status; status comes only from the inventory, and change of status only from a recorded decision.
6. **Repetition must do work.** An occurrence counts only if removing its sentence would change the story. Repetition that merely repeats is deleted, whatever it does for the numbers.
7. **Every chapter changes something.** A reading after which nothing is different — in the world, in knowledge, in feeling — is not a reading; it is padding with a title.
8. **Emotion is evidence.** Feelings are readouts of events the text has shown. A story may not buy affect it has not earned.
9. **Explanation is not a narrative move.** Neither characters nor narrators lecture. If the learner needs to understand something, the event, the image, or the apparatus carries it — never a speech.
10. **Images clarify; they never carry.** Nothing plot-bearing lives only in a picture, and no picture reveals what the text has not yet told.
11. **Simplify by narrowing the world, not by flattening the plot.** Fewer characters, fewer objects, fewer settings — not fewer causes and consequences. A three-beat plot with two characters is easy; a one-beat plot with six characters is not.
12. **Change engines, not nouns.** Variety between stories is variety of desire, obstacle, and payoff. Renaming is not variety.
13. **Naturalness is not negotiable; the escape hatch is the chunk.** When natural Arabic exceeds the envelope, admit a listed, fixed, non-productive chunk. Never contort the Arabic, and never quietly expand the envelope.
14. **Curiosity is a deliverable.** Every reading is accountable for the reason a learner turns the page. "It is good for them" is not a reason.
15. **Word counts are bands, not quotas.** Complete and causal beats long. Filler is a rejection, not a style note.
16. **An honest gap beats a plausible filling.** Missing facts are surfaced, never invented — in stories, in records, in evidence, in status. This applies to every artifact the program produces, including its own paperwork.
17. **A gate you can edit is not a gate.** Checks verify effects. Approval means a recorded human decision with stated scope — never a banner, a green run, or an assumption.
18. **Consistency is part of the product.** The learner's trust in book N+1 is built by books 1 through N. Every departure from series norms is either a planned progression step or a defect.
19. **Readers are books.** Every decision — prose, apparatus, design, tone — is finally tested against the question a real publisher asks: *would a reader who owed us nothing keep reading?*

# Part XII — Prohibited Practices

The following are prohibited in all readers, at all levels, permanently. Each is grounds for rejection wherever found.

**Story and prose**

1. Target sentences disguised as a story; readings organized around exhibiting language.
2. Grammar-driven stories — plots, scenes, or dialogue contorted to force a structure's appearance.
3. Dialogue that does not change the next action; decorative or teacher-style dialogue; drill questions in character mouths.
4. Random rescue, unmotivated object transfer, forced bargains, hidden transactions, load-bearing coincidence.
5. Generic "everyone is happy" endings as default; unearned emotion; closing mood lines with no resolved stake behind them.
6. Noun-swapped stories — the same engine re-costumed with new names and objects, within a reader or between readers.
7. Word-count padding; filler scenes; repetitive plot loops; redundant restatement inside a reading.
8. Implausible plot events at any level — the language envelope is never an excuse for a story that makes no sense.
9. Plot information carried only by illustrations; images revealing outcomes ahead of the text.

**Language**

10. Arbitrary vocabulary selection — any Arabic item without a traceable authorization; admission of items because they are "common," convenient, or wanted by a story idea.
11. Vocabulary drift — silent promotion, silent status change, support quietly carrying plot, recognition items quietly becoming required.
12. Fabricated or unverified frequency claims; citing a frame as a ranked item; citing a related form as evidence for its neighbor; conflating homographs; backfilling recorded evidence gaps.
13. Unsupported grammar — structures ahead of sequence without recorded exception; unlicensed forms; person/tense/number/polarity extensions by analogy; phrase targets used in pieces.
14. Translationese, calques, and pedagogically contorted word order; artificial dialogue; drill cadence in prose.
15. Dialect mixing and sub-standard spellings absent explicit governance; dialect evidence offered for MSA items.
16. Decorative, partial, or inaccurate vocalization; stripping vocalization for "authenticity"; first encounters with unvocalized forms; ambiguity created by orthographic choices.
17. Retyping approved Arabic anywhere downstream; diacritic-naive search against vocalized text.
18. Rare vocabulary deployed for artificial excitement.

**Difficulty and progression**

19. Uncontrolled difficulty increases — vocabulary dumps, unplanned complexity spikes, any dimension of difficulty jumping ahead of the coordinated progression.
20. Assuming language the ledgers do not establish; ignoring due recycling obligations; abandoning taught language without recorded retirement.
21. Support removed before strength arrives — fades imposed by aesthetics or schedule rather than earned by acquisition.

**Apparatus and product**

22. Textbook-unit thinking — organizing a reader around a syllabus, filling template slots, adding readings to cover items, or evading architectural ceilings by relabeling.
23. Disconnected exercises — activities unlinked to any reading; activities introducing substantial new language; comprehension work answerable without understanding; trivia quizzes.
24. Grammar terminology, paradigms, conjugation tables, parsing, or translation drills anywhere learners can see them.
25. Output demanded before input — unsupported free production ahead of the staircase; assessment of recognition-only language; production demands on input-first items without their conversion decision.
26. Fabricated cultural content; stereotyped or universalized portrayals; exoticizing ordinary life; requiring learners to disclose personal, religious, or family information.

**Record and process**

27. Drafting prose against unapproved briefs or plans; repairing early-stage failures with late-stage assets.
28. Citing draft documents as binding; carrying status labels forward without confirmation; treating relayed or remembered approvals as recorded decisions.
29. Inflated self-assessment in briefs and QC records; approvals without stated scope; findings suppressed to keep a schedule.
30. Proceeding across an unresolved source conflict; filling a source gap with invention.
