A successful CI/TPRS-based story is not simply "an easy story with repeated vocabulary." It is a story engineered so that learners receive dense, understandable, memorable exposure to a small amount of language while still wanting to know what happens.

The strongest ones usually have these features:

1. **A controlled linguistic core.** The story is built around a limited number of high-frequency target structures rather than a large thematic vocabulary list. New language is tightly controlled; previously acquired language does most of the work.
2. **High comprehensibility without linguistic distortion.** Learners should be able to follow the story through known language, context, gestures, visuals, cognates where appropriate, and carefully established support. But Arabic should remain natural; familiar grammar should not be forced to express meanings it does not normally express.
3. **One clear human want.** The protagonist wants something understandable at the learner's level: find a picture, give someone a gift, get somewhere, meet someone, solve a small misunderstanding. The want gives the repetitions a reason to exist.
4. **A concrete anchor.** A visible object, person, place, or situation carries the story question. Ideally it is specific enough to matter — this photograph, this gift, this missing book — not merely a random vocabulary noun.
5. **An early central question.** The learner quickly understands what remains unresolved: Where is the picture? Will Sami give Muna the gift? Who is Sara? Can she find the book? The story then withholds the answer long enough to create curiosity.
6. **A simple event chain.** Strong beginner plots often follow something like: want → attempt → information → action → failed result → new information → another action → discovery → resolution. This creates narrative movement without requiring complex grammar.
7. **Repetition caused by the plot.** Target structures recur because someone genuinely needs to ask again, search again, compare again, try another place, follow another clue, or perform another step. The learner should feel the situation repeating — not the textbook repeating.
8. **Variation within repetition.** The same structure appears with different people, objects, locations, or outcomes. This gives the learner repeated processing without producing identical sentences.
9. **A manageable information gap.** The learner knows enough to understand the problem but does not yet know the answer. New information arrives gradually. A good story controls what the protagonist, other characters, learner, and illustrations reveal.
10. **One or more meaningful failed attempts.** Failure is extremely useful in TPRS because it naturally generates repetition: searches → does not find → asks → gets another clue → searches again. But failure should arise logically, not because the author needs another repetition.
11. **A real turning point or discovery.** Something changes. A clue proves wrong, another character knows something, the object appears somewhere unexpected, or an earlier detail suddenly matters.
12. **A climax written mostly in already-owned language.** The most important moment should not contain a cluster of unfamiliar vocabulary. New language should generally be established before the climax so learners can experience the payoff rather than decode it.
13. **A genuine resolution.** The main want gets resolved. The learner can clearly say what happened and who has what, where everyone is, or how the problem ended.
14. **A small final resonance when possible.** The best endings do slightly more than say "and everyone is happy." They may contain a callback, visual echo, small reversal, recurring object, or gentle joke that makes the story memorable without introducing another plot.
15. **Dialogue that performs narrative work.** Characters speak because they need information, correct someone, respond to a misunderstanding, offer a clue, make a request, or change the situation. Dialogue should not exist merely to model a grammatical pattern.
16. **Natural spoken/written register.** Beginner prose should still sound like the target language. Avoid relentless sequences such as "Ali goes… Ali sees… Ali asks… Ali says…" when normal discourse permits verb-first clauses, pronoun continuation, or omitted repeated subjects.
17. **Emotion that arises from events.** The story should not constantly tell learners that characters are happy, sad, or calm. Where the language envelope permits, emotion can be shown through action, silence, reaction, dialogue, possession, movement, or visual behavior.
18. **Dignity and learner identification.** Characters may misunderstand, forget, fail, or make mistakes, but they should not be made stupid or humiliated simply to generate comedy. The learner should be able to identify with the difficulty.
19. **Simple situational humor.** Beginner humor works best through expectation and situation — not wordplay that requires advanced language. A mistaken identity, repeated failed attempt, unexpected object, or small visual payoff can be enough.
20. **A low support burden.** New support words should not carry most of the plot. If the critical clue or climax requires several unfamiliar words, the story probably exceeds the current language envelope.
21. **No hidden grammar that has not been taught.** If past tense is needed, use authorized past tense. Do not make the imperfect perform unnatural pseudo-past work merely to keep the story inside a grammatical ceiling.
22. **A relationship between grammar progression and story possibility.** New grammar should unlock better stories. Location language unlocks search plots; reason structures unlock motives; past tense unlocks recounting; contrast unlocks failed attempts; subordination unlocks richer causal relationships.
23. **Personalizability for oral TPRS.** Before or around the written story, the teacher should be able to personalize the core structures through students' names, preferences, objects, locations, and choices. The story should provide material for PQA and class-created variation.
24. **Questionability.** A good TPRS story naturally produces useful comprehension questions: who? where? what? does he have it? does she want it? is it here or there? why? what happens next? Questions should deepen comprehension rather than merely test memory.
25. **Circling without obvious drilling.** The story should permit repetition through questions, alternatives, negatives, confirmations, and personalized variations without the learner feeling trapped in a grammar exercise.
26. **A strong oral version before or alongside reading.** TPRS works especially well when meaning is first established through teacher talk, gesture, questioning, personalization, acting, or co-created storytelling before learners encounter the fuller written text.
27. **Visuals that support but do not spoil.** Images should clarify people, objects, locations, and actions while protecting important discoveries. The illustration should not show the missing object before the text finds it.
28. **Retellability.** After sufficient exposure, learners should be able to reconstruct the story with pictures, sentence starters, a word bank, gestures, or familiar language. A good beginner story has a clear enough structure to live in memory.
29. **Transfer beyond the story.** The target language should later appear in a new context: a parallel story, information gap, conversation, functional text, listening task, or supported writing activity. The story is an acquisition engine, not the final destination.
30. **Narrative independence.** The ultimate test is: *If the learner already understood every Arabic word, would there still be a reason to keep reading?* If the answer is no — if the story exists only to recycle the target — it is comprehensible input, but not yet a successful story.

**The structural formula:**
small human want → visible problem → motivated repetition → failed or partial attempt → new information → meaningful discovery → resolution → small resonance

**The broader TPRS formula:**
limited language + compelling meaning + repeated comprehensible exposure + personalization + interaction + memorable narrative = acquisition-rich story.

**Full standard — main and cumulative stories, Unit 2 onward.** Every criterion is a named line in the story's Gate B audit; the drafting session quotes each number with a one-line verdict (PASS / FAIL / N-A with reason). Criteria 6, 10, 11 (event chain, failed attempts, turning point) are the ones Unit 1's arrival-scale plot only lightly exercised — from Unit 2 they are load-bearing: **a main story without at least one logically-arising failed attempt and a real turning point fails its gate.** Criterion 26 is discharged by the platform's story-preview and input activities (the course's oral layer per SC-024); criterion 23's personalization material feeds the classroom phase and the notebook's parallel-story frames.

Must satisfy: **1, 2, 3, 4, 13, 16, 17, 18, 20, 21, 27, 30** — the language integrity, want/anchor/resolution spine, dignity, register, no-spoiler visuals, and the narrative-independence test. ## E. The tense law and the absurdity licence (SC-041)

**E-1 · Fixed tense slots, every unit.** ⟨**Reaffirmed and made explicit by SC-073, 2026-09-15**, which confirms the slots apply from Unit 1 onward in every unit, adds that Story 1 never uses the imperfect as pseudo-past narration, and preserves natural nonpast forms in Story 2. This section agreed with SC-073 already and is unchanged in substance.⟩ The unit's **first (main) story is narrated in the present**; the unit's **second reading is narrated in the past**. This is a slot, not a suggestion: the learner meets both narrative tenses in every unit, each always in the same place, so the contrast is systematic rather than accidental. It is the owner's own criteria 21 and 22 made structural — *"if past tense is needed, use authorized past tense"* and *"past tense unlocks recounting."*

**E-2 · Narration past, dialogue present.** In a past-tense reading the *narration* carries the past (دَخَلَ · قَالَ · رَأَى) while **dialogue stays in the present**, because that is what people speak. This is how narrative works in Arabic and in every other language, and it is what keeps the tense law from forcing كَانَ / لَمْ يَكُنْ into a Novice envelope before they are taught. A state that must be narrated in the past goes into someone's mouth instead, or the sentence is redesigned.

**E-3 · Absurdity is licensed.** A reading may break real-world logic for delight — a talking object, an impossible helper, a rule of the world quietly suspended — provided the **language envelope, the dignity rule (18), and the sensitivity law (P-7 / SC-027 / SC-036) hold without exception.** The absurd element is the *event*, never the Arabic. The owner's worked example, recorded as the reference shape: *a boy hunts a book through three places, fails at each, reaches the library, and the librarian turns out to be a talking cat who calmly tells him where it is while he stands there baffled.* The pattern to take from it: **three honest failures, then a turn that is impossible rather than merely surprising, and a protagonist left perplexed while the absurd party is perfectly matter-of-fact.**

**E-4 · Consequence for the generation prompt.** `READING_GENERATION_PROMPT.md` §3 bans "personified weather and objects (the sun does not smile; the door does not wait)." **E-3 supersedes that ban for this course's licensed-absurd readings** — a pen may speak. The ban still governs *decorative* personification: the difference is that an absurd element must be the story's engine or its turn, never a stray flourish in a line of description.

**E-5 · What absurdity does not license.** It does not license new vocabulary (the envelope is unchanged), untaught grammar, a character made stupid for the joke, or a discovery the reader cannot follow. Nonsense that the learner cannot picture is not absurdity — it is incomprehensible input.
