# Intaliq 1 — Calibration against a published CI/TPRS reader

*Editor's evidence file · 2026-09-16 · Sample supplied by the Program Owner: Blaine Ray, **Look, I Can Talk!** Student Book, Spanish, 2nd edition (Sky Oaks Productions; illustrated Greg Rowe; edited James J. Asher). Chapters 1–4 read in full; measurements below are on the printed story text of each chapter, transcribed from the book's own text layer and hand-corrected against the page image. The sample is used for **calibration only**. Nothing in it is borrowed — the Cast Bible forbids recognisable borrowing, and the one chapter whose shape matches Unit 2's brief (chapter 3) is the shape the Story Standard already records as the owner's own reference example (§E-3).*

---

## 1 · What the sample is, and what it is not

Each chapter has the same apparatus: a numbered picture-vocabulary page (13–19 items) → an eight-panel wordless picture strip → the boxed story → nine exercises (true/false · cloze · sequence · short-answer *"if the answer is not in the story, invent it"* · retell in your own words · picture-only *Versión A* and *Versión B* to retell · invent your own story · a page of one- to three-sentence recombination mini-stories to repeat). Twelve chapters in all.

**One structural fact governs every number below.** This is a TPRS *student* book: the story is told orally by the teacher, with gesture and questioning, *before* the learner reads it. The printed text is the record of a story the class has already lived. Intaliq's readings are **primary input** — the course has no teacher book (SC-045) and the platform's preview activities are its oral layer (SC-024). So the sample's tolerances are an **upper bound** on what Intaliq may allow itself, not a target.

### 2.3 Recycled share — the number the SC-085 escalation needed

Measured **by Intaliq's own LC-2 method** (production record §3): token count on the story text, proper names excluded, articles and clitic pronouns and the conjunction *y* excluded as glue (the analogue of الـ and وَ counted in their host), a token counted as recycled if its lemma appeared in any earlier chapter's story or vocabulary page.

For comparison, the naïve count that includes every article, pronoun and conjunction gives 56.5% · 70.9% · 59.0% — and even that never clears 71%, and only once clears 60%.

**Intaliq Unit 2, same method:** discarded drafts 41.0% / 46.3%; repair pass 52.4%; revision-4 drafts (§5 of the drafts file) **40.0% / 53.8%**.

**Finding.** A published, commercially successful CI/TPRS reader runs its second chapter at ~42% recycled and its fourth at ~36–46%. **LC-2's 70–85% floor is not a description of how CI readers are written at this stage; it is a description of a mature corpus.** Unit 2's failure to reach it is the instrument's failure, exactly as escalated at SC-085. The ramp recommended there (≥50% U2 · ≥60% U3 · 70–85% from U4) is, if anything, *stricter* than the sample at every point, which is appropriate given §1 — Intaliq's readings carry the input alone.

## 3 · What the sample does that Intaliq should take

### 3.1 The story shapes — and what makes them work at ninety words

**Chapter 1 (92 words).** Coqui has a big cat → Pedro, "a bad boy", grabs it and throws it to the ground → the cat escapes → Coqui cries *and cries*, Pedro laughs → Mónica sees her crying, walks over, gives her own cat → Coqui is very happy. Six beats, four emotional turns (loss · grief against laughter · kindness · joy), one villain, one kind stranger. It is melodrama, and it is not subtle — *and it is compelling at ninety words* because every sentence changes someone's state. Nothing is described that is not about to matter.

**Chapter 3 (151 words) — the Unit 2 shape.** Susana gets up: *¿Dónde está mi libro?* She looks in the corner — no. Runs to the chair, sits, looks — no. Sits on the floor, looks — no. Asks again. Her mother: *look in front of the window!* — no, *Mamá, no está aquí.* Her mother: *on the table, between the magazine and the pencil* — found. She runs to the door, shouts *adiós*, opens it, **leaves without closing it**, goes to school.

This is a *where-is-my-X* story, the very shape the owner rejected in Unit 2's first drafts — and it works where those did not, for four reasons that are all transferable:

1. **The want is real and clocked.** She is getting up; she is going to school; the book is needed *now*. The rejected drafts had an object nobody needed.
2. **The helper is wrong once.** Her mother's first instruction fails. That single wrong clue is the laugh, and it is also what makes the search feel like life rather than a list.
3. **The locatives are the spine, not decoration.** *en el rincón · en el piso · enfrente de la ventana · en la mesa · entre la revista y el lápiz* — every failure is one more locative, and the precision increases (place → surface → position between two objects). This is Unit 2's headline done as plot.
4. **A character note at the end.** She leaves the door open. It is not explained. It is the "small final resonance" of Story Standard 14, and it costs three words.

**Chapter 2 (207 words) — the name problem solved.** Zero proper names. The two characters are *el mono* and *la vaca*; the verb morphology and the role nouns carry every attribution. Chapter 3 uses one protagonist plus *ella* (×12) and *su mamá*. Chapter 1's 12% name density is the highest in the sample, and it comes from three same-gender-ambiguous characters — the same source as Unit 2's rule-19 problem (TF-3). **The sample's solution is cast design, not prose repair:** one protagonist, or two of different kind. Arabic can do the same with وَلَد / بِنْت and with قَالَ / قَالَتْ where the pair is mixed; where the pair is same-gender (as Unit 2's Story 2 now is, for a reason given in the drafts file), the name density will stay near 20% and rule 19 has to accept it.

### 3.2 The support budget

The sample's unlisted-word rate says that a CI reader treats a *picture-anchored* support word as nearly free. Intaliq's illustration plan (a numbered object page per unit, exactly as this book has) gives the same anchor. **The support budget of four per unit is tighter than the evidence warrants.** The revision-4 drafts use seven (four from revision 3 + بَيْت، غُرْفَة، مِفْتَاح, all three named in the scope map's own Unit 2 budget). That is still a third of what the sample does.

### 3.3 The exercise set

Ray's nine exercises map almost one-to-one onto the Activity Architecture's input tiers: true/false (recognition) · cloze (supported retrieval) · sequence (structure) · short answer with *invent it if not in the story* (personalisation — Standard 23) · retell (Standard 28) · picture-only variants A and B (Standard 29 — transfer) · invent your own · recombination mini-stories. Two of these Intaliq does not have and should consider: the **picture-only variant** (the same strip with one element changed — the cat becomes a dog — retold by the learner) and the **recombination page** (a dozen two-line stories from the same lexicon, explicitly *not* meant to be compelling, meant to be repeated). The recombination page is the honest answer to LC-6's encounter floors: it is where the eighth and ninth encounters live, so the story does not have to carry them.

## 4 · What the sample does that Intaliq must not take

**The cruelty engine.** Chapter 1's motor is a boy who throws a cat and laughs at a crying girl. It is the most efficient stakes-generator in the book and it is closed to Intaliq under Story Standard 18 (dignity), rule 16 of the Blueprint, Constitution VIII.6, and P-7. Intaliq's stakes have to come from *want* and *misunderstanding*, never from a character made bad. The revision-4 drafts are built accordingly.

**The oral dependency.** Every tolerance in §2 is a tolerance for a text the learner has already heard. Intaliq's readings cannot assume that, which is why the SC-085 ramp is kept below the sample's own numbers and why support stays at seven, not twenty.

**The open lexicon.** The sample glosses nothing and lists half. Intaliq lists everything — that is the whole point of the ledgers, and the calibration does not loosen it. What changes is the *size* of the listed support set, not the discipline of listing.

**The recombination mini-stories as readings.** Ray does not pretend his one-line stories are stories; they sit on a page headed *repeat the following*. Intaliq's discarded drafts were, in effect, recombination exercises printed in the story slot. The distinction the sample draws is the one the owner drew.

## 6 · Second sample (2026-09-16) — Adriana Ramírez, *Learning Spanish with Comprehensible Input — First Year*, "Historia extendida uno: Los gatos azules"

*Supplied by the owner as three page images (story, continuation, 22 comprehension questions). Counts below are by eye from the page and are approximate to ±1.*

**What it is.** A ~330-word present-tense "extended story" — the written-up form of a TPRS class story, with the class's absurd contributions kept in (a president of the cats, aged 110; a rival president aged 250; a blue elephant the boy already owns). Skeleton: character + two likes with reasons → want (ten blue cats; has one) → obstacle (none sold in Egypt) → authority 1 refuses → travels to India → authority 2 has them → price → purchase → home, *muy feliz porque*. Two scripted dialogues (greeting · *¿qué necesitas?* · request · yes/no · price · thanks · goodbye). Apparatus: 20 literal questions in text order plus two personal ones (*¿Dónde vives tú? ¿Qué te gusta?*).

**Repetition density, by eye.** *gatos azules* ≈ 23 times (one every 14 words) · *le gusta(n) / me gustan* ≈ 9 · *quiere / quiero tener* ≈ 6 · *presidente (de los gatos)* ≈ 8 · *porque* **5** · numbers 8 (ages, counts, a price). Every target structure appears affirmative, negative, as a question, and in first and second person inside the dialogues. Two paragraphs restate earlier paragraphs nearly verbatim (the elephant paragraph appears twice).

**Measured against the Story Standard.** Criteria 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 7 (partly), 8, 15, 23, 24, 25 — strong. Criteria 6, 10, 11, 14, 30 — weak: one refusal, no turn, no surprise, a predictable outcome from the moment India is named, and the ending is the "everyone is happy" close (Blueprint IX.3), rescued only by its explicit *porque*. Criterion 7 (repetition caused by the plot) is violated on purpose: the restated paragraphs are textbook repetition, not situation repetition. By the 24-point gate this text would not pass; by its own purpose — twenty exposures to three structures in a text a Novice can read alone — it succeeds completely.

**What it settles that Ray did not.** (1) **Reason structures again in a first story.** *Porque* five times, including in the emotional close (*feliz porque ya tiene diez*). Two published first-year readers now put *because* in their opening text; our map holds `لِأَنَّ` to Unit 7. (2) **Emotion stated, with its cause, once, at the end** — exactly the VIII.7 shape, and only there. (3) **Absurdity as the engagement engine at Novice**: the story has no turn, so the cat-president is doing all the work of making a bare skeleton memorable. Our E-3 licence exists and the current Unit 2 pair does not use it. (4) **A Story-1 regime.** At 330 words and 23 repetitions this is a different instrument from Ray's 92-word chapter 1 or our 90-word readings: a *builds-the-language* text. The Blueprint already gives Story 1 that role; this is what that role looks like when taken to its limit.

**What not to take.** The two-authority skeleton as a standing shape; the verbatim paragraph restatement (a native reader feels it as padding — Ray does not do it); the outcome being visible from the midpoint.

**Consequence proposed, not taken.** Let Story 1 grow into this regime deliberately — **150–250 words, each core structure ≥10 times across affirmative / negative / question / first / second person**, redundancy tolerated as narrative restatement rather than banned as textbook repetition — while Story 2 stays the tighter, turn-driven text at ~90–120. That is a Blueprint rule-25 and Standard-7 question and is the owner's.

### 6.1 The teacher's book (supplied in full, same day) — what the student page could not show

*Adriana Ramírez, **Teaching Spanish with Comprehensible Input through Storytelling**, 2nd ed. 2021, First Choice Books. Method pp. 7–25; unit plans for 16 stories; quizzes. Read: method in full, stories 1–3 with their vocabulary lists and personalised questions, the Unit Three overview.*

**Every story exists in two versions.** The *historia básica* (teacher's book, ~250–330 words) is the skeleton the class tells over three days, personalising names, ages, places and traits as it goes; the *historia extendida* (student workbook, read on day 5) is a second, longer telling of the same story with different details and new material — the author's stated ratio is **"80% comprehensible, 20% new."** Story 1's básica has Pablo, White Rock, Egypt, Argentina; its extendida has Carlos, Egypt, India, a blue elephant, and *porque* ×4.

**The 80/20 claim, measured.** The extendida's tokens against the básica plus its 38-item vocabulary list, by Intaliq's LC-2 method: **231 of 276 = 83.7% known**; 19 new lemma types (*porque, muy, grande, frío, inteligente, simpático, compra, regresa, quince, ciento, cincuenta…*). The author's ratio is accurate — **and it is a ratio between two tellings of one unit's story, not between a unit and the units before it.** That is the number LC-2's 70–85% band was written to resemble, and Unit 2's readings were being measured against the wrong denominator: Unit 2 Story 2 against Unit 1 alone scores 31%; **against Unit 1 + Story 1 + the phrase page it scores 72% by form and ~90% by lemma** (the only lexically new tokens are أَيْضًا ×3, لَكِ ×2, أَعْطَتْ ×1). Recommendation carried to §7.

**Vocabulary per story: 38–50 listed items, as phrases with glosses.** *hay un · se llama · tiene __ años · vive en · le gustan · quiere tener · necesita hablar · no tiene más · le dice que · le da · está feliz.* This is SC-038's phrase-label principle at ten times Unit 2's band, and the list is a *reading aid* (the learner writes it in a workbook before the story), not a productive target.

**Sequencing.** Stories 1–3 teach *has · likes · wants · needs to talk with · goes to · they sell · gives · says · is happy · because* — the want-and-exchange verbs first. Prepositions of place arrive at **story 15 of 16** (*dentro de, al lado de, debajo de, detrás de, encima de*), packaged with states of mood and school supplies. Ray puts the lost-book locatives at chapter 3. Two published sequences disagree on *where*; both put motion, transfer and *because* in the first three stories. This is the evidence behind SC-090's first application and behind the §4 recommendation in the drafts file.

**Personalised Questions (PQ).** Twelve to twenty-five second-person questions per story, cumulative across the year (*¿Cómo te llamas? ¿Qué te gusta? ¿A dónde vas? ¿Estás cansada?*), asked for 7–10 minutes at the start of every class. This is Blueprint rule 10's first/second-person exposure done as a standing routine rather than inside the story. Intaliq has no teacher; the platform's preview activities are the place for it, and the sample page's «وَأَنْتَ؟ وَأَنْتِ؟» block is the seed.

**Absurdity is method, not accident.** "Including bizarre and peculiar details during the storytelling also helps students make stronger connections" (p. 14); students supply names, ages, nationalities and *personality traits* — i.e. the class describes the characters. That is SC-089's principle (people introduced and described per story) and E-3's licence, both as standard practice.

**Oral first, confirmed.** Three days of telling, one of skits, and only then the reading, sentence-by-sentence translated in class. The upper-bound caveat in §1 applies to this sample at least as strongly as to Ray.

**What not to take.** Every story ends *está feliz* (the default close the Blueprint rejects); the noun-swap between básica and extendida is the exact template the Constitution's VIII.3 prohibits *as a second story* — it is legitimate here because the extendida is a second *telling*, and that distinction is the one Intaliq should keep: retellings live in the apparatus (Version A/B, the recombination page), not in the Story 2 slot.

## 7 · Consequences from the second sample, proposed and not taken

1. **Re-aim LC-2.** Measure Story 2 against *Unit 1–(N−1) + Story 1 + the unit's phrase page* with a **≥80% known** target (the sample's own ratio, met by Unit 2's current pair at 72% by form / ~90% by lemma); measure Story 1 against prior units **as a report only**. This dissolves SC-085 without a ramp and restores the band's original meaning.
2. **Story-verb precedence.** Two published first-year sequences put *want · have · go · give · say · because* before place; the §4 recommendation to re-declare Unit 2's headline as *where is it, and going there* stands, with the note that Ramírez would have gone further.
3. **PQ as a platform routine.** A cumulative second-person question set per unit, in the preview activities.
4. **Phrase page as reading aid, not target.** Sized by the reading's needs (Ramírez: ~40), listed with glosses, never assessed — which is what SC-090 already makes it.

*All four were taken: 1 → CR-1, 2 → SC-092 and SC-094, 3 → CR-16, 4 → CR-13 (SC-091).*

## 8 · The first reading written under the rules — the owner's verdict (2026-09-17)

The calibration exists to be tested against a reading. The Unit 2 pair drafted under CR-1–CR-16 (revision 5) was line-edited by the owner and returned with this assessment of Story 2: *"a warm, modestly engaging vignette — not a gripping adventure, but no longer merely a location drill."* Recorded here as the calibration's first outcome, unimproved: the rules moved the readings from drill to vignette; they did not by themselves produce a gripping story. What the owner's own edits added to Story 1 — `هَلْ`, `عِنْدَ الْبَابِ`, `يَنْظُرُ`, `وَلٰكِنْ`, a hand — were all **support words the rules would have let in and the drafter did not reach for**. The lesson belongs beside CR-4: *unit verbs first* is a discipline, not a ceiling; the reading is furnished until it reads well. Revision 6 carries the edits ⟨SC-095⟩.
